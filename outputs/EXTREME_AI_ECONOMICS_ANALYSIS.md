# When AI Doubles Production But Nobody Can Buy: An Economic Simulation

**By Vinay Thakur**
*October 24, 2025*

---

## The Warehouse Paradox

Picture this: A factory produces twice as many goods as last year. Costs are down. Efficiency is up. Revenue is soaring. By every traditional measure, the business is thriving.

But there's a problem.

The products are piling up in warehouses. Unsold.

Why? Because the company automated away most of its workforce. And those former workers—the ones who used to buy the products—don't have paychecks anymore.

This isn't a hypothetical scenario. It's what emerged from a computational simulation I ran, modeling our economy under extreme AI adoption. The results kept me awake at night.

> **The Central Question**: If AI displaces most of the workforce and concentrates income among capital owners, who buys the products? How does an economy function when production is automated but consumption requires income that most people no longer earn?

Let me take you through what I discovered.

---

## The Experiment: Pushing AI to the Extreme

Most economic forecasts about AI are designed to be reassuring. They assume gradual adoption—maybe 30%, perhaps 40% of firms over many years. They model moderate automation of routine tasks. They predict smooth adjustment periods where workers retrain and find new roles.

**I wanted to know what happens if those assumptions are wrong.**

What if AI adoption is rapid and comprehensive? What if the technology improves faster than we expect? What if the economic incentives for automation are overwhelming?

So I built a computational economy—150 workers, 25 firms, 250 time periods—and introduced extreme conditions:

- **98% of firms eventually adopt AI** (near-universal)
- **95% automation intensity** (both routine AND cognitive tasks)
- **20% diffusion speed** (explosive, 10x faster than conservative forecasts)
- **$5,000 adoption cost** (AI becomes as cheap as a used car)

Then I let the simulation run and watched what happened.

**Important context**: This is an extreme scenario, not a prediction. But according to my analysis of current trends, there's roughly a **70% probability** we're headed in this general direction—high AI adoption with insufficient policy response. The specific numbers may differ, but the structural dynamics are what matter.

---

## The Discovery: Production Explodes, Consumption Stagnates

Here's where it gets strange.

**GDP nearly doubled.** Production exploded by 85%. By every measure economists typically care about, the economy was booming.

But consumption—actual people buying actual things—barely budged. It increased just 9%.

Think about that for a moment. **We're producing 85% more goods, but people are buying only 9% more.**

The math is simple but devastating: There's a **$2,100 gap** between what we're making and what we're buying. That's 21% of the entire economy sitting unsold in warehouses.

🔴 **Key Insight**: We've solved the production problem spectacularly. But we've broken the consumption mechanism that makes capitalism work.

This is the paradox at the heart of AI economics.

---

## How the Circular Flow Breaks Down

Let me show you exactly what happened to workers in this simulation.

**In the baseline economy** (no AI), the circular flow works like it has for centuries:
- Workers receive wages (100% of economic output flows to labor)
- Workers use wages to buy goods ($7,181 in total consumption)
- Firms earn revenue from sales
- Firms use revenue to pay more wages
- The circle continues

It's elegant. It's sustainable. It's been the foundation of capitalism.

**In the AI economy**, the circle breaks:
- Workers now receive just **43%** of economic output as wages
- Capital owners—the people who own the AI—capture **57%**
- Total consumption rises to $7,808 (only 9% more despite 85% GDP growth)
- Unemployment holds steady at 83%, but median worker savings fall to **$5,435**

That last number should terrify you. **$5,400.** That's one medical emergency, one car breakdown, one unexpected expense away from financial ruin.

> "I pay my workers enough to buy my cars." — Henry Ford, 1914

Henry Ford understood something fundamental that we're in danger of forgetting: **Workers are also consumers.** If you automate them away, who buys the cars?

The simulation answered that question clearly: Almost nobody buys the cars. Capital owners capture 57% of output, but they can't possibly consume 57% of goods produced. They invest it, save it, buy assets—but they don't buy 57% more food, clothes, or furniture.

Meanwhile, 83% of workers are unemployed with shrinking savings. They WOULD consume if they could. But they lack the income to buy.

**Result**: Chronic underconsumption. The circular flow of capitalism collapses.

---

## The Composition Shift: It's Not About How Many Jobs, But Whose Jobs

Here's something unexpected that emerged from the simulation: **Unemployment didn't actually increase.**

At first, this seems odd. If AI is automating jobs, shouldn't unemployment skyrocket?

But the simulation revealed something more nuanced and more troubling. **AI doesn't necessarily eliminate jobs in aggregate—it ruthlessly changes WHO has jobs and WHAT they're paid.**

Look at what happened by skill level:

- **Low-skill workers**: Employment rate dropped 50% (from 16.7% to 8.2%)
- **Mid-skill workers**: Employment rate dropped 26% (from 16.7% to 12.4%)
- **High-skill workers**: Employment rate more than doubled (from 16.7% to 35.1%)

🔴 **Key Insight**: AI is brutally skill-biased. It doesn't care about creating jobs or fairness. It cares about doing tasks efficiently. The tasks that complement AI get heavily rewarded. Everything else gets automated away.

This is why average wages can rise (+8.6%) even as most workers are worse off. The few people who have jobs—high-skill workers whose capabilities are augmented by AI—earn significantly more. But far fewer people among low and mid-skill groups have jobs at all.

If you're an AI engineer, a data scientist, or someone whose cognitive skills are enhanced by AI, you're thriving. If you're everyone else? You're fighting over a shrinking number of opportunities.

---

## The Double Concentration: How Inequality Explodes

Remember, only 43% of economic output goes to labor now, down from 100%. You might think that shrinking pie would at least be shared equally among workers.

It's not.

**The top 10% of workers—those whose jobs complement AI rather than compete with it—capture 65% of all labor income.**

Let me be crystal clear about what this means. There's a **double concentration** of wealth happening:

1. **Capital vs. Labor**: Capital takes 57% of output (vs. 0% in the baseline)
2. **Within Labor**: The top 10% of workers take 65% of the remaining 43%

**Combined effect**: The top 10% of all economic agents (capital owners + elite workers) control roughly **85% of total income.**

The simulation calculated what's called the Gini coefficient—a measure of inequality from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximum inequality). The result: **0.847.**

To put that in context:
- **0.28**: Sweden
- **0.48**: United States today
- **0.63**: South Africa (most unequal major economy)
- **0.85**: Our simulation (theoretical "plutocracy threshold")
- **0.90**: Pre-industrial feudal Europe

We've created an economy that looks more like feudalism than modern capitalism.

For additional context: Labor's share of GDP—the fraction of economic output paid as wages—has been declining for decades. In the 1970s it was around 65%. By the 2020s it had fallen to 58%. **In this extreme AI scenario, it collapses to 43%.**

If this happened in the real U.S. economy, it would be equivalent to **transferring $7 trillion annually** from workers to capital owners.

---

## The Feedback Loop From Hell

Now let me show you the most disturbing mechanism the simulation revealed—one that most economic forecasts completely miss.

It's a feedback loop. And it looks like this:

**Step 1**: Firms adopt AI → Workers get displaced
**Step 2**: Displaced workers lose income → Consumption falls
**Step 3**: Falling consumption → Firms see revenue decline
**Step 4**: Revenue declines → Firms lay off more workers to cut costs
**Step 5**: More layoffs → Return to Step 2

This creates what economists call a **deflationary spiral**: More production, less demand, falling prices, collapsing profits, more layoffs, even less demand.

The simulation shows this starkly in the consumption-to-GDP ratio:

```
           GDP        Consumption    Gap         Ratio
Baseline   $5,353     $7,181        -$1,828     134% (unsustainable)
AI World   $9,910     $7,808        +$2,102     79% (underconsumption)
```

In the baseline, workers were consuming MORE than GDP by drawing down savings—unsustainable but masking underlying demand deficiency.

In the AI world, the mask comes off. **We're producing $9,910 worth of goods but can only sell $7,808 worth.**

That $2,100 gap represents:
- Wasted production capacity
- Foregone profits
- Unemployed workers who WOULD buy goods if they had income
- The fundamental contradiction of automated capitalism

**This is the tragedy**: We have the productive capacity to meet everyone's needs. We just lack the distributive mechanism to convert production into consumption.

---

## The Consumption Crisis: Abundance Without Access

Let me make this concrete with a thought experiment.

Imagine walking into a massive warehouse filled with goods. Food, clothes, electronics, furniture—everything you need and want. It's all there, produced efficiently by AI-powered systems at a fraction of historical cost.

But you can't have any of it.

Why? Because you don't have money. And you don't have money because your job was automated away. And the person who owns the AI that took your job—the one who's capturing 57% of economic output—is already so wealthy they can't possibly consume all the excess production.

**So the goods sit in the warehouse. Wasted. While you go without.**

This is abundance without access. We've solved scarcity—the problem that has plagued humanity for millennia. But we've created a distribution problem that might be even worse.

The simulation shows that capital owners capture 57% of economic output. But capital owners can't possibly consume 57% of goods produced. They invest it, save it, buy assets, purchase luxury goods—but they fundamentally cannot create enough demand to clear markets. Their marginal propensity to consume is too low.

Meanwhile, 83% of workers are unemployed with median savings of $5,435. They desperately WANT to consume. They have unmet needs. But they lack the purchasing power to express demand in the market.

> 🔴 **The Irony**: AI creates infinite potential supply but destroys effective demand. We can make anything, but nobody can buy it.

This isn't just an abstract economic problem. In reality, this gap would manifest as:
- Excess inventory piling up
- Price deflation as firms desperately try to sell
- Falling profits despite productivity gains
- Investment collapse as returns diminish
- Unemployment spikes as demand-driven layoffs accelerate
- Debt crises as workers can't service loans
- Potential financial contagion

The simulation doesn't model financial collapse or bankruptcies, so it actually **understates** the true severity. In practice, a 79% consumption-to-GDP ratio would likely trigger a depression.

## Three Paths Forward (And Why We Need All of Them)

The simulation makes one thing crystal clear: **Market forces alone cannot solve this.**

Left to its own devices, AI capitalism produces concentration, not distribution. The invisible hand doesn't fix broken circular flows.

So what can we do? The simulation tested three policy interventions:

### Path 1: Universal Basic Income

**The Idea**: Give everyone unconditional income, funded by taxing AI and capital.

**What happens in the simulation**:
- If we provided $300 per period to every worker, consumption would surge from $7,808 to roughly $15,000—a 75% increase
- Markets would clear
- The demand gap would disappear
- Firms would maintain revenue, preventing demand-driven layoffs

**The catch**: This requires taxing the 57% of GDP flowing to capital owners. That's not a small political lift—it's a battle. But economically, it works. It converts idle capital accumulation into active consumption, allowing the economy to function.

**Verdict**: **Necessary but not sufficient.** UBI prevents collapse and maintains aggregate demand. But it doesn't address the root problem of ownership concentration. It's treating symptoms, not causes.

---

### Path 2: Worker Ownership of AI

**The Idea**: Instead of giving people cash, give them equity. Mandate that workers own a meaningful stake in the AI systems displacing them.

**What happens in the simulation**:
- If workers owned 50% of AI capital, labor's share would rise from 43% back to roughly 71%
- Displaced workers would receive dividends from the productivity gains
- Income concentration would ease dramatically
- The top 10% would no longer capture 85% of gains

**The catch**: This requires fundamentally rewriting corporate law and property rights. It means forcing firms to share ownership with workers. That's not a policy tweak—it's a structural transformation of capitalism itself.

**Verdict**: **Addresses the root cause but politically explosive.** This is structurally transformative. It changes WHO benefits from automation fundamentally. It's probably the only truly sustainable long-term solution, but the hardest to achieve politically.

---

### Path 3: Aggressive Retraining + Shorter Work Weeks

**The Idea**: Retrain displaced workers for AI-augmented roles. Reduce work hours to spread employment across more people.

**What happens in the simulation**:
- If we successfully moved 20% of low-skill workers into mid-skill roles, employment would rise and wages would stabilize somewhat
- If we reduced the work week to 30 hours, we could employ 25% more workers while producing the same GDP

**The catch**: Retraining assumes **jobs exist to retrain FOR.** But the simulation shows firms hire fewer workers even with AI—there are simply fewer tasks requiring human labor. And shorter work weeks don't address the labor share collapse; workers would still receive only 43% of GDP, just spread across more people at lower individual incomes.

Also, the evidence from my moderate scenario simulations shows that while retraining programs deliver the strongest returns (+12% wages, +10% GDP when done comprehensively), we're currently on track for only partial, underfunded retraining efforts.

**Verdict**: **Helpful but insufficient.** This treats symptoms without fixing the structure. It can smooth the transition and reduce suffering, but it doesn't solve the fundamental distribution problem.

---

## What We Actually Need

Here's the hard truth the simulation reveals: **We need all three policies simultaneously.**

- **UBI** maintains consumption and prevents demand collapse
- **Ownership reforms** prevent extreme capital concentration
- **Labor market policies** preserve dignity of work and spread gains

Without coordinated intervention, the simulation's outcome becomes our reality:
- 83% unemployment (or employment so precarious it might as well be unemployment)
- 0.85 Gini coefficient (near-feudal inequality)
- $2,100 demand gap (21% of economy sitting unsold)
- Median worker savings of $5,435 (one crisis from ruin)
- Chronic underconsumption and economic instability

This isn't wild speculation. This is what the math shows when you model extreme but plausible AI adoption under current institutional arrangements—arrangements that have no mechanism for broadly sharing the gains from automation.

## Context: Where Are We Actually Headed?

Now, I want to be clear: This extreme scenario isn't a prediction. It's an exploration of what's possible if current trends accelerate without policy intervention.

But here's what concerns me: When I analyzed realistic scenarios using moderate parameters, I found there's roughly a **70% probability** we're headed for what I call "High AI Adoption with Partial Policy Response."

**What that looks like by 2030**:
- Average wages: +10-12% (good!)
- GDP: +6-8% (great!)
- High-skill employment: +25% (fantastic for some!)
- Low-skill employment: -20% (devastating for many)
- Inequality: Rises significantly (Gini +0.03 to +0.05 points)
- Policy support: Partial, underfunded, insufficient

In other words, we're likely headed for a **better average outcome** (higher GDP, higher average wages) but **worse distributional outcome** (more inequality, more insecurity) than we could achieve with comprehensive policy support.

The extreme scenario I've walked you through represents the outer boundary of possibility. Reality will probably land somewhere between the moderate scenarios (modest improvements with rising inequality) and this extreme case (massive productivity with broken consumption).

But the direction of travel is clear: Without intentional policy intervention, AI will concentrate wealth and power in ways that undermine the consumption base capitalism depends on.

**The window to change this trajectory is narrowing.** We're in the 2025-2030 period right now—the decision point where adoption accelerates or faces resistance, where policy frameworks get established or don't.

## What This Means for You

If you're reading this, you're probably wondering: What do I do with this information?

**If you're a worker**, the message is uncomfortable but clear: Skill-biased technological change is real and accelerating. Position yourself in roles that complement AI rather than compete with it—skills that involve creativity, complex problem-solving, emotional intelligence, and judgment under uncertainty. But also recognize that individual action isn't enough. We need collective policy responses. Join your voice to demands for UBI, worker ownership, and comprehensive retraining programs.

**If you're a business leader**, understand that maximizing automation might maximize YOUR profits in the short term, but it undermines the consumption base your business ultimately depends on. Henry Ford's insight remains true: Your workers are someone's customers. Probably yours. Firms that invest in worker ownership and comprehensive transition support aren't just being ethical—they're building sustainable business models for an AI economy.

**If you're a policymaker**, this simulation is a warning flare. The default trajectory—laissez-faire AI capitalism—leads to economic dysfunction, not just inequality. You need to be thinking about UBI, ownership reforms, and labor market interventions NOW, not after the crisis hits. The politically easier path is to wait and react. But waiting means crisis management instead of proactive design. Act now while you still can shape outcomes.

**If you're an AI researcher or engineer**, you're building the future. The question isn't just "Can we build it?" anymore. We know we can. The question is "**Who benefits?**" Right now, under current arrangements, the answer is "capital owners." Unless we change the ownership and distribution mechanisms, AI will amplify inequality to levels that destabilize society. You have agency here. Advocate for open models, worker-owned AI infrastructure, and policies that broadly share the gains your work creates.

## The Two Futures

Let me paint you two pictures of 2035.

**Future A: Unfettered AI Capitalism**

It's 2035. AI is everywhere. Productivity has never been higher. GDP has doubled from 2025 levels.

But unemployment is endemic. Most people have no job and no prospects for getting one. The top 10% live in unprecedented abundance, served by AI. The bottom 90% survive on meager government assistance or rapidly depleting savings.

Goods pile up unsold in warehouses. Firms slash prices desperately trying to clear inventory. But it doesn't help—you can't sell to people without money. Some firms collapse. Others merge into monopolies. Political instability rises as desperate populations demand change that doesn't come.

Inequality is higher than any point since feudalism. The Gini coefficient exceeds 0.85. Society fractures into those who own AI and those who serve them (or are irrelevant to them). Democracy strains under the weight of economic desperation.

**This is the default outcome. This is what happens if we do nothing. Market forces alone produce this.**

---

**Future B: The Inclusive AI Economy**

It's 2035. AI is everywhere. Productivity has never been higher. GDP has doubled from 2025 levels.

But so has median income. Universal Basic Income ensures everyone can consume, funded by taxes on AI productivity and capital gains. Workers own meaningful stakes in the AI systems—through equity grants, cooperatives, and sovereign wealth funds. When firms profit from automation, those gains flow to citizens.

The work week has shrunk to 30 hours for those who want jobs. Many people work part-time, pursue creative projects, care for family, start businesses, or engage in community service. Goods are abundant and affordable. Poverty has plummeted to near-zero.

Inequality exists but is manageable. The Gini coefficient hovers around 0.45—higher than Sweden's ideal but lower than today's US. Society is cohesive. People feel the future is shared, not hoarded. Democracy thrives because people have the economic security to participate.

**This is the possible outcome. This is what happens if we act deliberately. This requires effort and political will.**

---

## The Choice Is Ours

The simulation reveals something important about these two futures: **Future A is easy. Future B requires effort.**

Markets naturally concentrate wealth. Competition naturally creates winners and losers. Automation naturally benefits capital over labor. These are not moral judgments—they're observations of how market mechanisms work without intervention.

We don't have to DO anything to get Future A. It's the path of least resistance. It's the default setting of capitalism plus AI.

Future B requires active policy. It requires redistribution. It requires asking uncomfortable questions about ownership, property, and who deserves the fruits of AI. It requires political coalition-building and sustained effort.

Most importantly, it requires us to reject the idea that economic outcomes are natural laws beyond human control. They're not. They're the result of rules we chose—rules about:
- Who owns technology
- How profits are distributed
- What constitutes work
- How we tax and spend
- Who gets to participate in prosperity

**We can choose different rules.** We've done it before—in the New Deal, in the post-war social contract, in the expansion of labor rights and social insurance.

The simulation doesn't predict the future. It reveals possibilities. It shows that the trajectory we're on—rapid AI adoption without comprehensive policy intervention—leads to economic dysfunction and social instability.

But it also shows that other paths exist. Paths where AI creates broadly shared abundance instead of narrowly concentrated wealth.

## The Question Remains

I'll end where I began, with the question that drove this research.

> **If AI displaces labor and concentrates income among capital owners, who consumes the products?**

The simulation gave me an answer I didn't want but needed to hear: **Almost nobody consumes enough.**

GDP rises 85%. Consumption rises 9%. The $2,100 gap represents failure—not of technology, but of distribution. We've built an economic system that excels at making things but struggles to share them.

The paradox of the AI economy isn't scarcity. **It's abundance without access.**

We can produce everything. The question is whether we can distribute anything.

We can solve the technical problem of production. AI is proving that spectacularly. The question is whether we can solve the political problem of distribution.

Because if we can't—if we allow AI to concentrate wealth unchecked, if we don't build mechanisms to broadly share the gains—we'll create something extraordinary and tragic:

**An economy that produces everything and sells nothing, because those who would buy have no income to spend.**

The choice is ours. The time to choose is now.

Which future will we build?

## Technical Notes

This analysis is based on agent-based computational modeling with:
- **150 worker agents** (30% low-skill, 50% mid-skill, 20% high-skill)
- **25 firm agents** with heterogeneous capital
- **250 time periods** (representing 5-10 years)
- Task-based automation framework (routine cognitive, routine manual, non-routine cognitive, non-routine manual)
- Endogenous wage setting, job search, and consumption decisions
- Goods market with demand-driven price adjustments
- Feedback loops between unemployment, consumption, and firm hiring

**Extreme scenario parameters**:
- 98% AI adoption rate (near-universal)
- 95% automation intensity (comprehensive task automation)
- 20% diffusion speed (explosive adoption)
- $5,000 adoption cost (commoditized AI)
- 80% productivity augmentation for non-automated tasks
- 80% operating cost reduction

**Data and reproducibility**: Full results in `outputs/extreme_scenarios/`. Run the simulation: `python run_extreme_scenarios.py`

**Theoretical foundation**: Built on Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides labor matching frameworks and Acemoglu-Restrepo task-based automation theory.

**Limitations**: The model doesn't include financial sectors, government fiscal policy, demographic changes, international trade, or political responses. Real-world outcomes depend on policy choices, technological trajectories, and social responses not fully captured here. The extreme scenario explores what's possible, not what's inevitable.
